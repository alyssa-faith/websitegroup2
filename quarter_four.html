QUARTER 4

Most typhoons that hit the Philippines are usually formed in the Western Pacific Ocean because of the ocean’s warm water and surface winds, making a tropical typhoon. Then, the typhoon will end until it finds cooler water or land. It begins when the ocean’s water is warm, and the sea surface temperature is more than 27°C. Then, the heat from the ocean’s warm surface warms the air above it, causing it to create a low pressure zone. As the warm air continues to rise and cool, it slowly condenses into clouds. This cycle creates a significant low pressure zone. Wind begins to circulate and the air surrounding it gets drawn inward, creating the spiraling structure that we call a typhoon. The more heat, the more powerful the typhoon is, and once the typhoon hits a cooler area, land or water, that is when it ends. This is the case when Typhoon Ulysses was formed. Typhoon Ulysses formed in the Western Pacific Ocean on November 8, 2020.

Landforms and bodies of water affect typhoons depending on how much energy they give off and the kind of energy source they provide. Bodies of water such as oceans and seas have a great influence on the weather of continental masses. As it forms due to high temperature around the sea surface, the typhoon gains energy and strength making it stronger. However smaller bodies of water like lakes, rivers, ponds, and other fresh water do not usually increase or decrease the strength/energy of the typhoon. Landforms, on the other hand, such as mountains can weaken a typhoon by cutting off its supply of the warm water as it moves on land, and mountains can create a friction that slows down the wind and weakens the strength of the typhoon. Typhoon Ulysses, for instance, was formed in the West Philippine Sea due to the ocean’s warm water. When it made a landfall in Patnanungan, Quezon Province on November 11, 2020, it sustained a wind speed of around 150 km/hr (90 mph). After reentering ocean waters, the typhoon further intensified, reaching a wind speed of around 177 km/hr (110 mph). As it passed through the islands of Luzon, the intensity of Ulysses or Vamco dropped below the typhoon category. However, as it exits the Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR), it was redeclared as a typhoon and further intensified in the South China Sea. This supports the idea that when a typhoon passes through landforms, its intensity could decrease, while passing through bodies of water could potentially intensify the typhoon.

Typhoons can cause massive destruction. They bring strong winds, heavy rain, and storm surges that can lead to flooding, landslides, and damage to homes, buildings, and crops. Power outages, disrupted transportation, and loss of life can also happen, especially in low-lying or coastal areas. The impact can last long after the storm has passed, affecting communities’ recovery and daily life. Our group went on many brainstorming to find one solution to minimize the impacts of typhoons. Below are the brainstorming we did.

Our fishbone diagram illustrates the impacts of typhoons on people and their surroundings. On the upper part, it shows the damage typhoons can cause, such as the destruction of crops, damage to infrastructure, and economic disruption. On the lower part, it highlights the effects of these damages on people. Based on this, we proposed a solution: enforce a disaster resilience program that strengthens infrastructure, improve early warning systems and ensure fast response and recovery efforts in order to minimize damage, protect livelihoods, and stabilize the economy.

Our main topic is Typhoon. Typhoons can cause destruction, economic losses, landslides, and flooding. And our solutions for these four problems that typhoons have caused are; Destruction: Strengthening infrastructure, restoring coastal ecosystems, and flood insurance. Economic Losses: Reducing costs, seeking financial support, promoting/getting crop insurance. Landslides: Retaining walls and channels, modifying land slopes, and early warnings and preparedness, Flooding: Building flood barriers, installing flood gates or shields, improving drainage systems.

With the 5 Why's graphic organizer, we answered each of the 5 why's for why there was a huge flood in an area, they were because of heavy rain produced by the typhoon, the drainage wasn't big enough to handle the rain, some of the drainage systems weren't built for typhoons, the authorities didn't take floods into consideration when building the drainage systems, and the authorities weren't aware of the flood and there was a lack of long term planning. We also put our solution for it, which was "Put more investments/consideration when building a drainage system".

In the end, we chose the solution in the fishbone diagram. We think enforcing a disaster resilience program that strengthens infrastructure, improves early warning systems, and ensures fast response and recovery efforts to minimize damage, protect livelihood, and stabilize the economy after typhoons would be the most reasonable and realistic solution to mitigate the impacts of typhoons.
